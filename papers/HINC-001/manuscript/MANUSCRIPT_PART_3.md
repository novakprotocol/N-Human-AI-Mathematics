## 7. Hidden center and infinitesimal commutators

### Theorem 7.1 - the center of the even monoid

The scheme-theoretic center of `M_E` is the disjoint union of zero and identity:

\[
Z(M_E)=\{(0,0,0),(1,1,0)\}.
\]

#### Proof

Let `z=(x,y,e)` be universally central. Commuting with the valid point `(0,1,0)` gives `e=0`. Now base-change to `R[t]/(t^2)` and commute with `(0,0,t)`. Equality of the two products gives `(x+y)t=0`, hence `x=y`. The core equation becomes

\[
x(x-1)=0.
\]

This polynomial is separable in characteristic two because its derivative is `1`, so the central subscheme consists of the two reduced points `x=0` and `x=1`. The converse is immediate. QED.

Every reduced-base point of `M_E` has `e=0`, so the reduced point monoid is commutative. The universal monoid nevertheless has only two central points. Thus reduced points do not merely miss nilpotents; they give the wrong answer to the universal center problem.

### Theorem 7.2 - the even unit group

The unit group is

\[
G_E=\alpha_2\rtimes\mathbf G_m,
\]

with points `(a,c)`, where `a` is a unit and `c^2=0`, and multiplication

\[
(a,c)(b,d)=(ab,ad+c).
\]

Its commutator is

\[
[(a,c),(b,d)]
=
\bigl(1,(a+1)d+(b+1)c\bigr).
\]

Consequently,

\[
Z(G_E)=1,
\qquad
[G_E,G_E]=\alpha_2,
\qquad
G_E^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong\mathbf G_m.
\]

#### Proof

The unit condition forces `a` invertible and the second diagonal coordinate equal to `1`, yielding the displayed law. Direct multiplication with

\[
(a,c)^{-1}=(a^{-1},a^{-1}c)
\]

gives the commutator formula. All commutators lie in the normal subgroup `alpha_2`.

Universal centrality first forces `a=1` by commuting with arbitrary infinitesimal translations and then forces `c=0` after base change to a Laurent-polynomial algebra with a free unit parameter. Hence the center is trivial.

Conversely, after the faithfully flat extension adjoining a unit `t` for which `t+1` is also invertible, every square-zero element `n` is the commutator of `(t,0)` and `(1,(t+1)^{-1}n)`. Thus the fppf-derived subgroup is all of `alpha_2`. Quotienting by it leaves `G_m`. QED.

For every field extension `K/F`, `alpha_2(K)=0`, so

\[
G_E(K)=K^\times.
\]

Every ordinary field-valued unit therefore appears central and the group appears abelian, although its group-scheme center is trivial and its derived subgroup is nontrivial.

The odd monoid and odd unit group are genuinely commutative. This gives the strongest parity contrast:

```text
even ordinary points: commutative and apparently central
even universal scheme: noncommutative with trivial unit-group center
odd ordinary points and universal scheme: commutative
```

## 8. Idempotent schemes

For an affine monoid scheme `M`, write `\operatorname{Idem}(M)` for the equalizer of the square map and the identity map.

### Theorem 8.1 -- even idempotents

The even idempotent scheme is

\[
\operatorname{Idem}(M_E)
\cong
\operatorname{Spec}F
\sqcup
\alpha_2
\sqcup
\operatorname{Spec}F.
\]

The three components are:

1. the zero endomorphism on positive degree,
   `(\lambda,\delta,\eta)=(0,0,0)`;
2. the infinitesimal family
   `(0,1,\eta)` with `\eta^2=0`;
3. the identity `(1,1,0)`.

#### Proof

Squaring an even endomorphism gives

\[
(\lambda^2,\delta^2,(\lambda+\delta)\eta).
\]

Hence the idempotent equations are

\[
\lambda^2=\lambda,
\qquad
\delta^2=\delta,
\qquad
(\lambda+\delta-1)\eta=0,
\]

in addition to the monoid equations.  The first two equations split the base into the four pairs `(0,0)`, `(0,1)`, `(1,0)`, `(1,1)`.  The relation `\lambda(\delta-1)=0` removes `(1,0)`.  At `(0,0)` and `(1,1)` the coefficient of `\eta` is a unit, so `\eta=0`; at `(0,1)` it vanishes and only `\eta^2=0` remains. QED.

### Theorem 8.2 -- odd idempotents

The odd idempotent scheme is the disjoint union of four reduced `F`-points:

\[
(0,0,0,0),
\quad
(0,1,0,0),
\quad
(1,1,0,0),
\quad
(1,1,1,1).
\]

These are respectively:

1. the zero positive-degree endomorphism;
2. projection onto the subalgebra generated by `a`;
3. projection onto the `\epsilon,a` head;
4. the identity.

#### Proof

Composition is coordinatewise, so idempotence requires each coordinate to be idempotent.  The defining equations then reduce to the four displayed solutions.  Exact ideal decomposition gives the product of four copies of `F`, so there is no nilpotent thickening. QED.

### Remark 8.3 -- comparison with algebraic-semigroup theory

Brion proves that the idempotent scheme of a commutative algebraic semigroup is finite and reduced.  The odd monoid is reduced and commutative, and its four-point idempotent scheme fits that theorem.  The even monoid lies outside those hypotheses: it is both nonreduced and noncommutative.  Its reduction has three reduced idempotents, while the full scheme thickens the middle one to `\alpha_2`.

## 9. Geometric and arithmetic fingerprints

### 9.1 Normalization of the common core

The normalization of

\[
C=V(x(y-1))
\]

is the disjoint union of its two affine-line branches:

\[
\widetilde C\cong\mathbf A^1\sqcup\mathbf A^1.
\]

On coordinate rings,

\[
F[x,y]/(x(y-1))
\hookrightarrow
F[x]\times F[y],
\qquad
f\mapsto(f(x,1),f(0,y)),
\]

and the image consists of pairs agreeing at the crossing. The conductor is supported at `(0,1)`.

The even monoid has reduction `C`; the normalization of its reduction is therefore the same two-line disjoint union. The odd monoid is already reduced and has normalization

\[
\mathbf G_m\sqcup\mathbf A^1\sqcup\mathbf A^1.
\]

### 9.2 Tangent dimensions

At the crossing `(0,1)`, write `v=y-1`. The core equation is `xv=0`, which has no linear term, so

\[
\dim T_{(0,1)}C=2.
\]

The even square-zero coordinate adds one tangent direction:

\[
\dim T_{(0,1,0)}M_E=3.
\]

At the global identity of the even unit group, the relation locally forces `y=1` while the square-zero direction survives, so the tangent dimension is `2`. The odd global identity lies on the smooth `G_m` component and has tangent dimension `1`.

These are the infinitesimal dimensions already visible in the automorphism group schemes.

### 9.3 Clifford-monoid picture on algebraically closed points

The reduced core points form a commutative inverse monoid with three idempotent levels:

1. the global group `G_m` on the branch `y=1`, `x!=0`;
2. a local group `G_m` on the branch `x=0`, `y!=0`, with identity `(0,1)`;
3. the zero `(0,0)`.

The odd construction adds a new global unit group above this three-level core. The even construction instead thickens the middle geometry infinitesimally and destroys commutativity without changing reduced points.

### 9.4 Finite-field counts and zeta functions

Let `q` be a power of two. For every `n>=1`,

\[
\#C(\mathbf F_{q^n})=2q^n-1.
\]

The nilpotent `alpha_2` coordinate has no nonzero field-valued points, hence

\[
\#M_E(\mathbf F_{q^n})=2q^n-1.
\]

The odd unit doubling contributes a disjoint `G_m` component:

\[
\#M_O(\mathbf F_{q^n})=3q^n-2.
\]

Both unit groups have exactly `q^n-1` field-valued points. Thus ordinary unit counts cannot see the even infinitesimal noncommutativity.

The Hasse-Weil zeta functions are

\[
Z(C,T)=Z(M_E,T)=\frac{1-T}{(1-qT)^2},
\]

and

\[
Z(M_O,T)=\frac{(1-T)^2}{(1-qT)^3}.
\]

The equality `Z(M_E,T)=Z(C,T)` is another precise expression of nilpotent invisibility to field-valued counting.

## 10. Finite-Artin point counts

Let

\[
R_n=\mathbf F_2[t]/(t^n).
\]

### Lemma 10.1

The following cardinalities hold:

\[
|R_n^\times|=2^{n-1},
\]

\[
|\{x\in R_n:x^2=0\}|=2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor},
\]

and

\[
|\{(x,y)\in R_n^2:x(y-1)=0\}|
=(n+2)2^{n-1}.
\]

#### Proof

A unit is exactly a polynomial with constant coefficient one.  Frobenius gives

\[
\left(\sum_i x_it^i\right)^2
=\sum_i x_it^{2i},
\]

so square-zero elements are precisely the multiples of
`t^{\lceil n/2\rceil}`.

For the crossing count, the case `x=0` contributes `2^n`.  A nonzero element of valuation `i` occurs in `2^{n-i-1}` ways, and its annihilator has `2^i` elements.  Summing over `0\le i<n` gives

\[
2^n+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{n-i-1}2^i
=2^n+n2^{n-1}.
\]
QED.

### Theorem 10.2 -- point-count formulas

For every `n\ge1`,

\[
|M_E(R_n)|
=(n+2)
2^{n-1+\lfloor n/2\rfloor},
\]

\[
|(M_E)_{\mathrm{red}}(R_n)|
=(n+2)2^{n-1},
\]

and

\[
|M_O(R_n)|
=(n+3)2^{n-1}.
\]

The idempotent counts are

\[
|\operatorname{Idem}(M_E)(R_n)|
=2+2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor},
\]

\[
|\operatorname{Idem}(M_O)(R_n)|=4.
\]

#### Proof

For `M_E`, choose a crossing pair `(\lambda,\delta)` and then an independent square-zero `\eta`.  For `M_O`, the local ring `R_n` has no idempotents other than zero and one, so the clopen decomposition gives the disjoint sum of the `R_n^\times` unit component and the crossing component.  The idempotent formulas follow from Theorems 6.1 and 6.2. QED.
